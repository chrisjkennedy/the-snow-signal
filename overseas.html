Going Overseas

A week in the Alps often costs less than a week in Colorado.

This is the least intuitive thing on the site, so it leads with the arithmetic rather than the argument. The flights are genuinely more expensive. Everything else is cheaper, and the gap on lift tickets is large enough to pay for the flight several times over.

The comparison

Why the gap is this large

It is almost entirely the lift ticket. American destination resorts have spent a decade pushing walk-up window prices past $250 a day, and the season pass products that undercut them (Epic, Ikon) only pay off if you commit before the season starts and then actually go. European resorts never made that move. A six-day lift pass in the Trois Vallées — the largest linked ski area in the world — costs less than two days at Park City.

Lodging and food follow the same pattern for a less obvious reason: European resorts sit in towns that existed before the skiing did, so there is a real economy with competition in it. A Colorado resort base is usually a single development with one owner setting every price.

The flight is the one line that goes the other way, and it is worth being honest that airfare is also the most volatile number in the budget — it can swing further than everything else combined. The case here holds at typical advance-purchase fares. It does not hold if you book late.

The chalet, and what "board" means

The biggest single saving available to an American skier in Europe is the catered chalet, and it is the part of the system that has no real US equivalent — which is why most people never consider it. Three arrangements, and the words matter because they are used loosely in marketing:

Catered chalet

Sometimes sold as "full board"

You book a bed, or the whole building, and staff live in and cook. The standard week is breakfast, afternoon tea when you come off the hill, and a three or four course dinner with wine included, on six of the seven nights — staff take one night off, and you eat out.

Roughly £795–£1,750 per person per week in France, depending heavily on how smart the chalet is.

Why it wins: dinner with wine every night is the line that would quietly cost $80–150 a head in a US resort town. Booked as a group taking the whole chalet, the per-head number drops again, and you get the building to yourselves.

Half board

Hotels, mostly

Breakfast and dinner included, every night, but no afternoon tea and usually no wine. Lunch is on you either way, because you are on the mountain.

The default for Austrian and Swiss hotels, and normally the cheapest way to eat two meals a day without cooking.

Where it wins: smaller groups and couples, where a whole chalet does not make sense and you would rather not self-cater. Austria in particular does this very well and very cheaply.

Self-catered

Apartment or chalet, no staff

You get keys and a kitchen. In France this usually means a résidence apartment, which is compact by American standards and near-universally ski-in.

Around £600 per person per week is a fair average for the accommodation itself, before food.

Where it wins: longer stays, families with young children who eat early, and anyone who would rather spend the money on lift days than on dinner. The saving is real but smaller than it looks once you have shopped for a week.

Chalet pricing is quoted per person per week and moves sharply with the calendar. Christmas, New Year and the February half-terms are the expensive weeks; mid-January and the second half of March are the cheap ones, and in a strong El Niño winter the late-season window is also when the Atlantic pattern has historically been most favourable to the southern Alps.

Practical notes

  • Transfers are not free. Geneva to the Tarentaise is two to three hours and a real cost. Budget €80–120 each way per person for a shared transfer, less if you split a car.
  • Lift passes are area passes. Buying the Trois Vallées or Espace Killy pass rather than the single-resort one costs a little more and multiplies the terrain enormously.
  • Ski hire is cheaper too, and generally better organised — most European resorts have shops at the lift base that store your gear overnight for a small fee.
  • Epic and Ikon do reach Europe, but partially, and the days are capped. Check the specific resort on the season planner before assuming your pass covers it.
  • The saving is largest for groups. A whole-chalet booking for eight to twelve people is where the arithmetic becomes lopsided.
